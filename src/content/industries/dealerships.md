## Three buildings, one site

A dealership is unusual because it contains environments with genuinely incompatible standards, and customers move between them.

The showroom is a retail environment held to a presentation standard closer to a jewellery shop than a garage. The workshop is an industrial environment with fluids, waste streams and safety requirements. The lot is an exterior surface carrying constant low-speed vehicle traffic and doing most of the marketing after dark.

Almost all the maintenance thinking here is about keeping those three from contaminating each other - literally in the case of the workshop, visually everywhere else.

## The showroom is on display all day

There is no acceptable version of a cleaning trolley beside a car a customer is considering. The showroom is on show for the entire trading day, which means the work is done before opening or after close, and anything longer belongs to a planned closure.

Glass is the dominant surface and the one that shows everything. It is large, it is constantly touched, and it sits directly behind the product. A dealership with marked glass looks neglected in a way that no amount of interior detail compensates for.

Floors are the other permanent conflict. A polished finish photographs beautifully and picks up tyre marks from the moment a vehicle is driven onto it. The realistic answer is a finish that tolerates rubber transfer and repeated cleaning, plus a shorter cycle on the vehicle entry path than on the rest of the floor - which is cheaper than restoring a whole floor that has dulled unevenly.

## The lot sells after dark

A meaningful share of viewing happens when the site is closed, from the road, in the evening. What the lot lighting does to paint finish is therefore a sales variable rather than a facilities one.

Uniformity and colour temperature matter as much as raw brightness. A lot with mismatched fittings, or with dark sections between poles, makes identical inventory look cheaper and makes the site feel less secure - which affects whether anyone stops during the day too.

Because these fittings need access equipment to service, they are worth treating as a batched project rather than replacing individually as they fail. That is the same logic as high-bay lighting in a warehouse, applied to a surface where the payoff is commercial rather than operational.

Surfaces and markings sit alongside it. Constant low-speed manoeuvring wears markings faster than through-traffic does, and faded bays on a display lot make the inventory look badly arranged even when it is not.

## The workshop has a waste stream

Service bay cleaning is not showroom cleaning done more roughly. Oil, coolant, brake fluid and washdown water are regulated, and they cannot enter surface drainage.

That makes the interceptor or separator a maintenance item in its own right, and it makes the cleaning method a compliance question - flushing a bay toward the nearest gully creates a discharge problem, not a clean floor.

Degreasing is also a safety item. A workshop floor with fluid on it is a slip hazard for technicians moving around vehicles and equipment, and it is the sort of thing that is noticed only after someone falls.

## Manufacturer standards are audited

Franchise programmes commonly specify finishes, colours, signage condition and presentation standards, and they are checked.

This makes ordinary repair decisions slightly less ordinary. A wall repainted in a near-enough colour, or a light fitting replaced with one that looks different, can create an audit issue costing more than the original job.

It is the strongest argument on any of these pages for keeping a written specification of what was used where. We keep it for painted areas by default, and where a manufacturer standard applies it is worth extending that to fittings and signage too.
