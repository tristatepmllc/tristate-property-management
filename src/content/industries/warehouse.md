## The failures cluster where things move

An industrial building is mostly a large, simple envelope. It does not fail evenly - it fails at the small number of points where mass is in motion.

Dock doors and levellers are hit constantly by vehicles and by loads. Floor joints take concentrated load from small hard forklift wheels. Traffic routes wear their markings away. Yard surfaces and drainage take HGV weight and standing water.

The roof, the walls and the racking mostly sit there. So a maintenance programme that treats the building as one uniform asset spends in the wrong places, and one that concentrates on the movement points spends far less for a better result.

## Access is the cost, not the part

The single most useful economic fact about a warehouse is that most work is priced by how hard it is to reach, not by what is being done.

A high-bay light fitting is inexpensive. Getting to it needs a lift, a trained operator, and the aisle below cleared of stock and traffic. That last part is a conversation with operations, not a booking.

Everything follows from this. Lamp changes should be batched rather than done singly. LED retrofits pay back faster here than the energy saving alone suggests, because they remove access events as well as watts. Anything else needing height - roof drainage, ductwork, signage - should be grouped into the same visit as the lighting rather than triggering its own lift hire later.

## Floors fail at joints

Warehouse slabs rarely fail in the middle. They fail at joints and edges, and the mechanism is specific: a forklift wheel is small and hard, so the entire load passes across the joint at a point.

Once an arris starts to spall it accelerates, because every subsequent crossing removes another fragment and the impact gets sharper. A joint repaired early is a small, quick job. The same joint left for a year is a spreading area of broken concrete in the busiest part of the building, and it damages the trucks crossing it as well.

Line marking sits alongside this. It fades under exactly the same wheel traffic, and faded separation between vehicle routes and pedestrian walkways is a safety issue long before it is a tidiness issue.

## Shifts, not opening hours

Offices are empty at night, retail is empty at night, and industrial sites frequently are not.

Where a site runs multiple shifts there may be no window that is genuinely quiet, so work is planned by zone rather than by time - one aisle, one dock, one area released at a time while the rest keeps running.

That makes the operations manager the person the schedule is actually built with. Booking a date without them produces a crew standing next to a lift they cannot deploy, and the wasted visit costs the same as a productive one.

## Docks are on the critical path

A dock position out of service is different from most faults, because the cost is per hour and it is immediate. Trucks queue, slots slip, and the knock-on runs through the whole day.

That makes levellers and doors the clearest candidate on any industrial site for scheduled servicing rather than reactive repair. They are hit constantly, they are mechanically simple enough that wear is predictable, and the cost of a failure at the wrong moment is out of all proportion to the cost of maintaining them.
