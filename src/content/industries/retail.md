## Maintenance is merchandising

In most building types, maintenance protects an asset. In retail it also directly affects revenue, which changes how the spending decision should be made.

A dulled floor, a storefront with fingerprints, a display bay lit at a different colour temperature from its neighbours, a restroom that feels neglected - none of these are faults in an engineering sense. All of them reduce how long people stay and how much they trust the operator.

That is why the work that matters most in retail is often the work that would be considered cosmetic anywhere else, and why it belongs on a schedule rather than in a reactive queue.

## The window is short and it has a hard edge

Retail trades through the times when offices are empty, and it trades at weekends. The maintenance window is a few hours overnight, and unlike an office it has an absolute finish time: the doors open, with customers behind them.

The practical consequence is that scope for a single night has to be sized conservatively. A job that overruns in an office means a slightly untidy morning. The same overrun in retail means equipment on a trading floor, a wet surface in a public area, or a unit that cannot open.

Anything that genuinely cannot be finished and cleared inside one window should be planned as a weekend or a scheduled closure from the start, not attempted and abandoned at 8 AM.

## The car park is half the customer experience

It is the first thing a customer sees and the last, it is the largest surface the site owns, and it is where the majority of public liability claims begin.

Three things drive it. Surface condition, where standing water and potholes are the same problem at different stages, because water sitting on asphalt accelerates its failure and freezes in winter. Markings, which fade gradually enough that nobody notices until bays are being ignored. And lighting, which affects both evening footfall and how defensible a trip claim is.

All three are cheap on a schedule and expensive after an incident.

## Circulation routes wear differently from everything else

The most useful thing to understand about a retail floor is that wear is not evenly distributed. The route from the entrance through the centre takes a multiple of the traffic that the edges take.

Treating the whole floor to the same maintenance schedule therefore over-serves most of it and under-serves the part everybody actually looks at. Concentrating burnishing and restoration on the circulation routes costs less and produces a better-looking centre, and it also means the periodic deep work can be shorter and fit the window.

## Seasonal peaks set the whole calendar

Everything above has to be arranged around the fact that for several weeks a year, nothing can be done at all.

Floor restoration, exterior cleaning, car park marking and any lighting work needing access all require time and drying, and none of it can happen during a trading peak. Work not completed before the peak does not get squeezed in - it waits for the quiet period afterwards, in a centre that is at its busiest and looking its worst.

Planning that backwards from the peak, rather than forward from today, is the single scheduling decision that most affects how a retail site looks all year.
