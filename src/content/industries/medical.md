## The record is the deliverable

Every building type on this site wants the cleaning done. A clinic wants it done and provable, and that changes how the whole contract is structured.

At an accreditation review, the question is not whether the building is clean today. It is what was cleaned, when, using what, by whom, throughout the period being reviewed. Work that was not recorded is functionally work that did not happen.

So the log is not a quality-control nicety here as it would be in an office. It is a compliance document, and it is the reason we run clinical work off a written protocol with a signed record rather than off a general cleaning specification.

## The practice's protocol wins

Most facilities are happy for a contractor to bring its own products and methods. Clinical settings frequently are not, and for good reason - the practice has been assessed against a specific protocol and is not free to vary it.

That typically means named products with specified dwell times, colour-coded equipment kept separate between clinical and non-clinical areas, and a defined order of working so a lower-risk area is never cleaned after a higher-risk one with the same equipment.

We take that as given rather than as a starting point for suggestions. Where we do have an opinion, it is about sequencing and staffing so the protocol is actually achievable in the time available, because a protocol that cannot be completed in the window is the most common way standards slip.

## Sessions, not opening hours

Clinics do not have an evening in the way an office does. They have sessions, and the space between them is short and full.

Periodic and deep work therefore goes outside session hours entirely, which in practice means evenings, a half-day closure or a weekend. That part is straightforward.

The difficult category is a fault during clinical hours - a blocked sink, a plumbing problem at a dental chair, a failed light in a treatment room. Each of those takes a room out of use, and a room out of use is a cancelled appointment.

What helps is deciding in advance which slots can be released for maintenance and how much notice that needs, rather than negotiating it while a patient is waiting.

## Waiting areas do a different job

The clinical areas are governed by protocol. The waiting room and the restrooms are governed by patient perception, and they carry more weight in a clinic than the equivalent spaces do elsewhere.

Patients cannot assess clinical standards. They assess the parts they can see and generalise from them, which means worn seating, a marked wall, tired flooring or a restroom that feels unattended is read as evidence about the care itself.

It is worth budgeting those areas as part of the clinical impression rather than as general upkeep, because that is how they are actually being interpreted.

## Where we stop

Clinical and sharps waste is not ours and should not be anyone's who is also doing general cleaning. It is a separately regulated stream with its own containers, licensed carriers and documentation, and keeping it fully outside the cleaning scope removes any possibility of the two crossing.

The same applies to anything requiring a licensed abatement contractor, which in older converted practice buildings comes up more often than people expect.
