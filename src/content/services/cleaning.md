## What actually goes wrong with cleaning contracts

Almost nobody switches cleaning companies because of one bad night. They switch because the scope was never written down properly, so six months in nobody can say whether the job is being done or not.

The usual pattern: the contract says "general cleaning of office areas". The crew rotates every few weeks because the contractor is short-staffed. Skirting boards, vents and the underside of desks quietly stop being touched, because nobody wrote down that they were ever included. The building slowly gets dirtier in the places nobody looks, and then someone important visits and notices.

Every part of how we run this is aimed at that specific failure.

## The scope is the product

Before we quote, we walk the building and write down what is in it: rooms, floor types, restroom fixture counts, glass, kitchen or break areas, entrance matting, and anything unusual. That list becomes the scope, and the scope becomes the checklist.

The frequency sits next to each item. Daily for restrooms and high-touch surfaces. Weekly for detail dusting and interior glass. Monthly or quarterly for vents, high dusting and edges. It is not a marketing exercise - it is what stops a job quietly shrinking.

## Floor care is where buildings are won or lost

Hard floors and carpet are the largest single thing a cleaning programme either protects or destroys. Vinyl composition tile that is burnished on a schedule lasts years past its replacement date. The same floor cleaned with the wrong pad and no sealant is grey and scratched in eighteen months, and the cost of replacing it dwarfs anything ever saved on the contract.

Carpet works the same way. Traffic lanes take the wear first, and hot water extraction on a sensible interval restores them. Left too long, the fibre is abraded rather than dirty, and no amount of cleaning brings it back.

We quote periodic floor work separately and schedule it in advance, rather than folding it into the monthly rate. That way you can see what it costs and when it happens, instead of finding out it was never being done.

## Restrooms decide how clean the building feels

A restroom is the one room where every visitor inspects the work closely. It is also the fastest place for standards to slip, because it needs both frequency and detail - fixtures, grout, touchpoints, dispensers, and the floor edge where cleaner tends to pool.

Restocking is part of it. Running out of paper on a Thursday tells everyone in the building that nobody is paying attention, regardless of how good the actual cleaning is.
