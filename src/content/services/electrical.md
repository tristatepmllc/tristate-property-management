## The trade where the cheap quote costs the most

Electrical work is largely invisible once it is finished. A junction box buried above a ceiling tile, a circuit run in undersized cable, a neutral shared between two circuits - none of it announces itself. It sits there working until load increases or something ages, and then it becomes an insurance question.

This is the one area where we will not price against an unlicensed quote, because the two things are not the same product. What you are buying is the part you cannot inspect.

## Fault finding, before parts

Most electrical calls start as a symptom rather than a diagnosis: a breaker that trips, a bank of lights that flickers, an outlet that has stopped working, a burning smell nobody can locate.

Working backwards from the symptom is the job. A flickering fitting can be the lamp, the driver, a loose neutral, or voltage drop on an overloaded run - and each has a different cost. Replacing parts in sequence until the symptom stops is expensive and does not tell you whether the underlying cause is still there.

## Panels tell you what the building has been through

An older distribution board is a record of every change made to the building. Circuits added and abandoned, labels that no longer match, breakers of mixed vintages, and load distributed unevenly across the phases because each addition was made in isolation.

Two things usually come out of looking at it properly. First, whether load balancing alone would solve the tripping, which is cheap. Second, whether the board itself is at capacity, which decides whether any future addition - a kitchen fit-out, a tenant's equipment, EV charging - is even possible without upgrading.

Knowing which of those you are facing is worth having before you plan a project, not during it.

## Lighting is a maintenance decision more than an energy one

The usual pitch for LED is the electricity bill. For buildings that run their lights continuously that is the right argument and the numbers are straightforward.

For everyone else the stronger case is access. Changing a lamp at ground level costs almost nothing. Changing one at high-bay height means a lift, a crew, and often a section of the building out of use while it happens. Cutting the number of those events per year can be worth more than the power saved, and it is the part that never appears in a payback calculation.

Emergency lighting sits in the same category for a different reason: the units are only useful if the battery still holds, and the only way to know is to test them and write it down.

## Working around an operating building

Most commercial electrical work needs something switched off. The planning question is what, for how long, and when it hurts least.

We would rather schedule a shutdown out of hours and do the work properly than improvise around live equipment during trading. Where the building genuinely cannot go dark, the work is staged by circuit so the loss is contained to one area at a time.
