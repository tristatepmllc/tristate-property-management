## The list nobody gets around to

Every building has one. A door that sticks. Two ceiling tiles stained from a leak that was fixed months ago. A shelf that needs remounting. A sign hanging crooked. A cupboard door off its hinge. A patch of drywall dented by a trolley.

None of them is worth a phone call on its own. That is precisely the problem - each item is individually too small to act on, so the list grows until the building visibly looks neglected, and by then it is a project rather than an afternoon.

The whole point of how we scope this work is to make the list actionable while each item is still small.

## Priced per visit, not per task

Small repairs are dominated by overhead. The drive, the parking, signing in, finding the right person, walking to the area, getting tools out - on a twenty-minute job that is most of the cost, and it is paid again for every separate callout.

Booking a visit rather than a task changes the economics completely. Send us the list, we scope the time it needs, and the items get worked through in order of priority until the visit is used. Anything that turns out to be bigger than expected gets flagged rather than silently absorbing the whole slot.

For buildings with a steady trickle of small items, a recurring visit on a fixed interval works better again, because nothing waits more than a few weeks and the list never grows into a project.

## Doors are the item worth taking seriously

They come up more than everything else combined, and they are the one category where a small fault is not cosmetic.

A closer that has lost its adjustment means a door that does not latch. On an external door that is a security problem. On a fire door it is a compliance problem, because the door only does its job closed. On a heavily used internal door it is the difference between a hinge adjustment now and a frame repair later, because a dropped door tears its own strike plate out over time.

Most of these are fifteen-minute fixes at the point somebody notices them and much larger ones six months later.

## Where we stop, and why that is useful

Some things are not handyman work, and pretending otherwise is how buildings acquire dangerous improvisations. Anything structural, anything needing a permit, anything a licensed trade must sign off on, and anything involving regulated materials goes to the right person.

The advantage of having those trades in-house is that saying "this is actually electrical" does not end the visit. It changes who comes back, on our schedule, on the same work order, with the same person accountable for the outcome. You do not go back to the start of the process with a new contractor.
