Property managers in Connecticut typically charge **8-12% of collected monthly rent** for long-term residential rentals, plus a leasing fee of 50-100% of one month's rent each time a new tenant is placed. On a $2,000/month rental with one tenant turnover a year, the realistic all-in cost is roughly **$3,400-$4,400 annually**, not the $1,920-$2,880 the headline percentage suggests.

That gap between the advertised percentage and what you actually pay is the single most common surprise for new landlords. This guide lists every fee you are likely to encounter, what each one is for, and which ones are worth pushing back on.

## What is the standard property management fee?

The core management fee is a percentage of rent, and 8-12% is the range across most of the US residential market for long-term rentals. Flat monthly fees exist too, usually $100-$200 per unit, and suit high-rent properties where a percentage would be disproportionate.

Where you land in that range depends on four things:

- **Property type.** A single-family house is simpler than a twelve-unit building with shared systems, and prices differently.
- **Portfolio size.** Owners with several doors negotiate toward the low end. One unit rarely does.
- **Rent level.** A $1,200 rent may attract a higher percentage or a flat minimum, because the work does not scale down with the rent.
- **Service scope.** Full-service management costs more than rent collection alone.

Short-term and vacation rentals are a different market entirely, commonly 20-40%, because turnover, cleaning and guest communication are constant rather than occasional.

## Is the fee charged on rent due or rent collected?

**On rent collected.** This is the most important line in the agreement and the easiest one to skim past. If the fee is calculated on rent *due*, you keep paying during a vacancy and while a tenant is in arrears. If it is charged on rent *collected*, the manager only earns when you do, which aligns their incentive with filling the unit and chasing late payments.

Ask the question directly and get the answer in writing. A manager who charges on rent due has no financial reason to hurry.

## What other fees should I expect?

The management percentage is the headline. These are the line items that make up the rest of the bill.

| Fee | Typical range | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| Leasing / tenant placement | 50-100% of one month's rent | Marketing, showings, screening, lease preparation |
| Lease renewal | $100-$300 or a small % of rent | Renewal paperwork and any rent adjustment |
| Maintenance markup | 10-20% on third-party invoices | Coordinating and overseeing repairs |
| Inspection | $75-$200 per visit | Periodic condition reports |
| Eviction handling | A few hundred dollars, plus legal costs | Court filings and administration, not attorney fees |
| Vacancy fee | Often a reduced flat amount | Charged while the unit is empty, if the contract allows it |
| Setup / onboarding | $150-$500 one-time | Bringing the property onto their system |
| Early termination | $500-$2,000+ | Exiting the agreement before the term ends |

Two of these deserve particular attention.

**The leasing fee is the biggest one people underestimate.** At 75% of a $2,000 rent, that is $1,500 the first time a tenant is placed. If your property turns over every year, it is $1,500 every year, and it dwarfs several months of management fee.

**The maintenance markup is where a lot of margin hides.** A 20% markup on a $600 repair is $120 for making a phone call. Companies that employ their own technicians can quote the work directly rather than marking up someone else's invoice, which is worth asking about specifically.

## What does a real year actually cost?

Here is the arithmetic on a $2,000/month single-family rental in Connecticut, managed at 10%, with one tenant turnover during the year and a couple of ordinary repairs.

| Item | Calculation | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Management fee | $2,000 x 10% x 11 collected months | $2,200 |
| Leasing fee | 75% of one month's rent | $1,500 |
| Maintenance markup | 15% on $1,800 of repairs | $270 |
| Two inspections | $125 each | $250 |
| **Total** | | **$4,220** |

That is 17.6% of the $24,000 in annual rent, against a headline rate of 10%. The number is not hidden or improper - every line is standard - but it is the number to compare offers on, not the percentage.

Rerun the same table with no turnover and it drops to about $2,700. Vacancy and turnover, not the management percentage, are what actually move the total.

## Which fees are negotiable?

More than most owners assume. In order of how often they move:

1. **Early termination.** Push for a 30-60 day notice period with no fee. This is the clause that traps people.
2. **Leasing fee.** Very negotiable on multiple units or an easy-to-let property.
3. **Advertising fee.** Usually should be inside the leasing fee, not on top of it.
4. **Technology or portal fee.** A $5-$30 monthly charge for an owner login is a nuisance line item. Ask for it to be removed.
5. **Setup fee.** Frequently waived to win the business.
6. **The management percentage itself.** Least movement here, and squeezing it too hard tends to buy you worse service rather than a better deal.

## How to compare two quotes properly

Percentages are not comparable across companies because the fee structures underneath them differ. Do this instead:

- Ask each company for their fee schedule in writing, not a headline rate.
- Build the table above with your own rent and your own realistic turnover.
- Ask whether maintenance is performed by employees or subcontracted, and what the markup is either way.
- Ask what the average days-on-market is for their listings. A manager who fills a unit three weeks faster than the other is worth more than a one-point difference in fee.
- Read the termination clause before anything else.

A manager charging 12% with in-house maintenance, no markup and a two-week average vacancy can easily cost less over a year than one charging 8% with a 20% markup and a six-week average vacancy.
