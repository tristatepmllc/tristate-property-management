The questions that actually separate property management companies are about **numbers and contract terms**, not the list of services - because every company offers roughly the same services. Ask for **average days-on-market, the full fee schedule in writing, and the termination clause**, and you will learn more in five minutes than an hour of discussion about their approach.

Here are fourteen questions worth asking, grouped by what they reveal.

## Questions about performance

These are the ones with numbers attached. A company that runs well knows these figures; a company that does not, hesitates.

**1. What is your average days-on-market?**
The most important question on the list. Vacancy costs more than the fee on most properties - a month empty on a $2,000 rental is $2,000, roughly ten months of management fee at 10%. A manager who fills units three weeks faster than another has paid for themselves before doing anything else. If they will not give you a number, that is the answer.

**2. What is your tenant retention or renewal rate?**
Every turnover costs vacancy plus make-ready plus leasing. A company with a high renewal rate is saving you that repeatedly. Anything reported as "very good" without a figure is not a figure.

**3. How many units do you manage, and how many per staff member?**
Portfolio size tells you about stability. Units per staff member tells you about responsiveness. A company managing 800 units with four people will not answer your call the way one managing 200 with the same team does.

**4. What percentage of your tenants are evicted in a year?**
This is a screening quality question in disguise. A low figure suggests they select carefully. A high one suggests they fill units to start the fee clock.

## Questions about money

**5. Can I have your complete fee schedule in writing?**
Not the headline percentage. The schedule: management, leasing, renewal, inspection, maintenance markup, setup, vacancy, eviction handling, early termination. Then build your own annual total using your rent and your realistic turnover. Percentages are not comparable across companies; annual totals are. There is more on this in [how much property managers charge](/blog/how-much-do-property-managers-charge/).

**6. Is the management fee charged on rent due or rent collected?**
It should be on rent **collected**. On rent due, you pay them during vacancies and while a tenant is in arrears, which removes their financial reason to hurry on either.

**7. Do you mark up maintenance invoices, and by how much?**
Markups of 10-20% on third-party work are common and legitimate if disclosed. What you want is the number in writing, because an undisclosed markup is the most common source of owner disputes.

**8. Do you employ technicians or subcontract everything?**
This is not a right-or-wrong answer, but it changes the incentives. In-house technicians can be dispatched and priced directly. A subcontracting model adds a coordination layer and usually a markup on top of someone else's invoice.

**9. What is the repair threshold I have to approve?**
There should be one - commonly $200-$500 - above which they contact you. No threshold means work happens on your account without your knowledge. A very high threshold means the same thing with extra steps.

**10. When do I get paid, and what does the statement show?**
A specific date each month, and a statement itemising rent, fees and every expense with invoices attached. "Around the middle of the month" and a summary total are both answers worth pressing on.

## Questions about the contract

**11. What is the termination clause?**
Read this before anything else. You want a **30-60 day notice period with no penalty**. Watch for early termination fees of $500-$2,000, automatic renewal, and lock-in periods of 12-24 months. This is the clause that turns a bad fit into an expensive one.

**12. Who holds the security deposit, and where?**
In Connecticut the deposit must sit in escrow at a Connecticut financial institution, earn annually published interest, and be returned or itemised within 21 days. Ask specifically how they track anniversary interest across tenancies - it is the requirement most commonly missed. See [Connecticut security deposit rules](/blog/connecticut-security-deposit-rules/).

**13. Are you licensed and insured, and can I see the certificate?**
Ask for the certificate rather than the assurance. Confirm general liability and, where relevant, errors and omissions coverage.

**14. Can I speak to an owner who left you?**
Every company can produce a happy client. The revealing request is for one who left. How they handle the question - and whether they will engage with it at all - tells you more than the reference would.

## What answers should worry me?

| Answer | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| "We don't really track days-on-market" | They are not measured on the thing that costs you most |
| "Our fee is all-inclusive" with no schedule | The fees exist; you will meet them later |
| "You won't need to worry about repairs" | No approval threshold |
| "Everyone signs a two-year term" | The termination clause is where their retention comes from |
| "We can start today" with no property visit | They are pricing your unit without seeing it |
| Any figure given as a feeling rather than a number | They do not have the number |

## How to compare two companies properly

Do not compare the percentages. Do this:

1. Get both full fee schedules in writing.
2. Build the same annual table for each, using **your** rent and **your** realistic turnover rate.
3. Add the vacancy difference: multiply the gap in their average days-on-market by your daily rent.
4. Compare the termination clauses side by side.
5. Then compare the totals.

A company at 12% with fast letting, in-house maintenance and no markup often costs less over a year than one at 8% with a 20% markup and six-week vacancies. The headline rate is the least informative number in the conversation.

## One thing worth doing before you call anyone

Work out what your **last turnover actually cost** - the vacant days, the make-ready, the lost rent. Most owners have never calculated it, and it is the number that makes the rest of this comparison concrete rather than theoretical.
