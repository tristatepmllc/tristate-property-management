Price your rental against **what comparable units are letting for right now**, not what yours rented for last year and not what you need it to earn. The right number is the one that produces **several qualified applicants within two weeks**. If you have no enquiries after ten days, the price is wrong - the market has already told you, and every week you spend arguing with it costs about **2% of the annual rent**.

Here is a method you can run in an afternoon.

## Why does vacancy beat rent level?

Because empty weeks are absolute and a rent increase is incremental. Compare the two directly.

| Rent | Extra $50/mo over a year | Cost of 1 month vacant |
|---|---|---|
| $1,500 | +$600 | -$1,500 |
| $2,000 | +$600 | -$2,000 |
| $2,500 | +$600 | -$2,500 |

Asking $50 too much and taking an extra month to let gains $600 and costs $2,000. You have to hold that tenant for **more than three years** just to break even on the decision - and even then only if they never leave.

This is why "we can always come down" is expensive advice. The reduction happens weeks later, after the listing has lost its freshness.

## How do I build a comparable set?

Five filters, in this order.

1. **Distance.** Start within a mile. Widen only if you cannot find five comparable units, and stop at the point where the neighbourhood genuinely changes.
2. **Bedrooms and bathrooms.** Match exactly. A 2-bed and a 3-bed are different markets, not adjacent points on a curve.
3. **Condition.** Be honest. A renovated kitchen is not comparable to yours if yours is original, however well maintained.
4. **What is included.** Parking, heat, hot water, water, snow removal, laundry. A unit at $1,900 including heat can be more expensive to occupy than one at $2,050 without it.
5. **Status.** Active listings tell you what is being asked. Recently let units tell you what was accepted. Both matter, and if the gap between them is wide, the market is asking more than it is getting.

Five to eight genuine comparables is enough. Twenty loose ones are worse than five tight ones.

## How do I adjust for what makes my unit different?

Take the median of your comparable set, then adjust with a defensible amount rather than a feeling. Typical directions:

**Upward:** off-street parking, in-unit laundry, recent kitchen or bathroom, central air, outdoor space, a top-rated school district, utilities included.

**Downward:** third-floor walk-up, no parking, dated finishes, shared laundry, small closets, a busy road, tenant pays all utilities.

Write each adjustment down with a number. If you cannot justify a number to yourself, you will not justify it to an applicant either, and the listing will sit.

## What does the market response actually tell me?

The listing is not a guess once it is live. It is a live test with a clear read.

| Signal in the first 7-10 days | What it means |
|---|---|
| Several enquiries, 2+ applications | Priced right, possibly slightly under |
| Steady enquiries, no applications | Price is close; something in the listing or the showing is losing people |
| Few enquiries, no applications | Priced above the market |
| Immediate flood, application day one | Priced under - note it for the renewal, do not withdraw the listing |

The most useful discipline here is a decision point. **Set a date before you list** - typically day 10 - at which you will act on what the market said. Without one, listings drift for six weeks while the owner waits for the right tenant who was never going to appear at that price.

## Should I raise the rent on a sitting tenant?

Review annually. Increase carefully. The relevant comparison is not "market rent versus current rent" - it is the increase versus the full cost of losing the tenant.

A turnover on a $2,000 unit realistically costs:

| Item | Typical |
|---|---|
| Vacancy, 3 weeks | $1,385 |
| Make-ready: clean, paint, small repairs | $600-$1,500 |
| Leasing / marketing | $0 self-managed, or 50-100% of a month |
| **Total** | **$2,000-$3,400** |

Now the increase. Going from $2,000 to $2,100 gains **$1,200 over a full year**. If that increase is what makes a reliable tenant leave, you have spent $2,000-$3,400 to win $1,200 - and you have replaced a known tenant with an unknown one.

That does not mean never increase. It means:

- A **modest increase a good tenant accepts** usually beats a full increase they refuse.
- Review every year so the gap never grows large enough to force a painful correction.
- If you are **more than about 10% below market**, the increase is worth the risk - and worth explaining, with the comparables, in writing.

For month-to-month tenancies, Connecticut requires proper written notice before a rent increase, and the notice period depends on the tenancy. Check the requirement for your arrangement before sending anything.

## What if the property genuinely will not let?

If ten days pass with no serious interest, the price is one of four causes and usually the first.

1. **Price.** Reduce to the median of your comparables, not by $25.
2. **Photographs.** Dark, cluttered or few. This is the cheapest fix available and the most commonly neglected.
3. **Availability.** If you can only show on Saturday mornings, you are choosing from whoever is free on Saturday mornings.
4. **The listing itself.** Missing utilities, parking, pet policy or square footage means people scroll past rather than ask.

Fix the listing before you cut the price twice. A repriced listing with the same three dark photographs performs about as badly as it did before.

## The short version

- Price against active and recently let comparables, not last year's rent.
- Five to eight tight comparables beats twenty loose ones.
- Adjust with written numbers, not instinct.
- Set your decision date before you list.
- Several applicants in two weeks means you got it right.
- Review sitting rents annually; weigh the increase against the full cost of a turnover.
