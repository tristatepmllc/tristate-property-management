Budget **1% of property value per year** for maintenance as a starting figure, then adjust for age: buildings over 30 years old commonly need **1.5-2%**, and recently renovated ones can run under 1%. On a $350,000 Connecticut single-family rental that is **$3,500 a year at the base rate**, or around $5,250 if the systems are older - and that figure sits alongside a separate capital reserve, not instead of one.

The rules of thumb all disagree with each other. Here is what each one is actually for.

## Which budgeting rule should I use?

Three are in common use and they answer different questions.

| Rule | How it works | Best for | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1% rule** | 1% of property value per year | A quick annual maintenance figure | Ignores age and condition entirely |
| **50% rule** | ~50% of gross rent covers all operating costs | Screening a purchase | Not a maintenance budget - it bundles taxes, insurance, vacancy |
| **Per square foot** | $1-$2 per sq ft per year | Larger or multi-unit buildings | Needs local cost data to be accurate |

For a single rental, use the 1% rule adjusted for age, and sanity-check it against per-square-foot. If the two land within a few hundred dollars of each other, your number is reasonable.

On a 1,600 sq ft house worth $350,000:

- 1% rule: **$3,500**
- Per sq ft at $1.50: **$2,400**
- Adjusted for a 1960s build: **$5,250**

The spread between those is the honest answer - budgeting is a range, and the age adjustment matters more than the method.

## What actually drives the number up?

Five factors, roughly in order of impact.

**1. Age of systems, not age of building.** A 1920s house with a new boiler, new roof and updated wiring costs less to run than a 1995 house where all three are original. Track system age, not construction year.

**2. Tenant turnover.** Every turnover triggers cleaning, paint and small repairs whether anything broke or not. Budget $600-$1,500 per turnover on top of routine maintenance.

**3. Climate.** Connecticut winters put freeze-thaw stress on roofs, gutters and exterior seals, and heating systems work hard for months. Heating service is not optional here in the way it might be further south.

**4. Property type.** Multifamily buildings add shared systems - common heat, roofs, parking, snow removal - that a single-family rental does not have.

**5. Who does the work.** Calling a different contractor for each job means list pricing and waiting. Standing relationships or in-house crews price differently, particularly on small jobs where a call-out charge is most of the invoice.

## What should a repair reserve hold?

**Enough to cover the largest plausible single failure without borrowing.** For most single-family rentals that is **$5,000-$10,000**.

The logic is not about the annual average. It is about timing. Your annual maintenance figure might be $3,500, but a boiler does not fail in monthly instalments. A reserve that only covers the average leaves you financing the one event the whole budget exists for.

Typical single-event costs to size against:

| Failure | Rough order of cost |
|---|---|
| Boiler or furnace replacement | Several thousand |
| Roof section repair | Low thousands |
| Water heater | Under a thousand to low thousands |
| Sewer line repair | Several thousand |
| Full interior repaint after a long tenancy | $1,500-$3,000 |

Fund the reserve first, then treat the annual maintenance figure as the operating budget on top of it.

## How do repairs and capital expenses differ?

**A repair restores something to working order. A capital expense replaces a system with a multi-year life.** Budget them separately or every replacement year will look like a catastrophe against a normal year.

Repairs: a leaking valve, a section of drywall, a failed switch, a broken window pane, an annual boiler service.

Capital: a roof, a boiler, a kitchen, windows, a driveway, a full rewire.

Capital items are predictable in a way repairs are not, because they have known service lives. Write down each major system, its age, and its expected remaining life. A roof at year 18 of a 25-year life is a budget item with a date on it, not a surprise.

## Does preventive maintenance pay for itself?

On mechanical systems, yes - and the mechanism is worth understanding, because it is not mainly about the equipment.

**Scheduled work happens at standard rates.** An annual heating service is a weekday appointment at a normal hourly rate. The same system failing on the first cold weekend is an emergency call-out at a premium, with a tenant without heat and a habitability obligation running.

**Small problems stay small.** A condensate line cleared during a service costs almost nothing. The same line blocked and overflowing through a ceiling is a plumbing job, a drywall job, a paint job and a tenant relations problem.

**Systems last longer.** Filters, coils and belts are the difference between a system reaching its expected life and reaching two thirds of it.

A reasonable baseline for a Connecticut rental:

- **Annually:** heating service before the season, gutters cleared, exterior seals and caulking checked, smoke and CO detectors tested, water heater checked.
- **Twice yearly:** filters, plumbing under sinks, roof and flashing from ground level.
- **Every tenancy:** full condition inspection at move-in and move-out.

## What does a realistic year look like?

A $2,000/month, 1,600 sq ft Connecticut single-family rental, mid-age systems, one turnover.

| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| Routine and reactive repairs | $2,200 |
| Heating service | $250 |
| Gutters, exterior, seasonal | $400 |
| Turnover: clean, paint, small fixes | $1,100 |
| **Total maintenance** | **$3,950** |
| Capital reserve contribution | $2,000 |
| **Total set aside** | **$5,950** |

That is about 16% of the $24,000 annual rent for maintenance, or 25% including the capital contribution. Owners who budget nothing and pay as things break are not spending less - they are spending the same money without knowing it in advance, and borrowing when the timing is bad.

## The short version

- Start at 1% of value; add 50-100% for older systems.
- Sanity-check against $1-$2 per square foot.
- Hold $5,000-$10,000 in reserve, sized to the largest single failure.
- Budget capital items separately, with their remaining service lives written down.
- Service mechanical systems on a schedule; it is cheaper than the emergency version of the same job.
- Add $600-$1,500 per turnover.
