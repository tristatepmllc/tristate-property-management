Deposit disputes are decided by **evidence, not argument**. A landlord with dated move-in photographs, a signed condition report and a contractor invoice wins; a landlord with a recollection and a round number does not - and in Connecticut, getting the return wrong can make you liable for **twice the deposit**. The whole protection costs about **90 minutes per tenancy**.

This is the documentation process, in the order you actually do it.

## What do I document at move-in?

Before the tenant takes the keys, and ideally with them present.

**Photographs.** Every room from two opposing corners, so nothing is out of frame. Then close-ups of:

- Every existing defect, however minor - the scuff, the chip, the mark on the counter
- Every appliance, including inside the oven and refrigerator
- Under every sink
- Window frames, sills and any condensation staining
- Flooring in each room, especially at thresholds and traffic paths
- Walls behind doors and in corners
- The meter readings
- Keys handed over, laid out and counted

**Photograph the things that look fine too.** Proving a wall was undamaged at move-in is exactly as useful as proving it was damaged - and you cannot know in advance which one you will need.

Make sure the camera's date stamp is on, and keep the originals rather than compressed copies sent over a messaging app.

**A written condition report, signed by both parties.** Room by room, with a condition note per item. Give the tenant a copy and keep the signed original.

A useful addition: give the tenant **72 hours after move-in** to add anything they notice and return the amended form. It costs you nothing, it catches things you missed, and it makes the report something they participated in rather than something they were handed.

## What is the difference between damage and wear?

This is where nearly every dispute actually sits. The line is deterioration from ordinary use versus deterioration from misuse, neglect or accident.

| Generally normal wear (not deductible) | Generally damage (deductible) |
|---|---|
| Faded or slightly scuffed paint | Holes in walls, crayon, unapproved paint |
| Small nail holes | Large anchors or unfilled fixings |
| Worn carpet in traffic paths | Burns, tears, pet stains |
| Loose door handles, worn hinges | Broken doors, frames or windows |
| Minor grout and caulk wear | Cracked tiles, water damage from neglect |
| Appliances failing through age | Appliances damaged by misuse |
| Dusty blinds | Broken or missing blinds |

Time matters as much as condition. Carpet that is worn after five years is wear; the same carpet worn after eight months is not. If you are deducting, be prepared to explain the expected life of the item and how much of it was consumed.

Deducting the full replacement cost of a nine-year-old carpet is the classic overreach that turns a defensible claim into a losing one.

## What should the move-out process look like?

Three steps, spread over the last month.

**1. The pre-move-out walkthrough, about two weeks ahead.** Walk the unit with the tenant and identify anything that would be deducted. Then tell them - because a tenant who repairs a hole or cleans an oven themselves is doing work you would otherwise pay for and deduct.

This step is skipped constantly and it is the highest-value one. Most deposit disputes are not disagreements about facts; they are reactions to surprise.

**2. The move-out inspection, after the unit is empty.** Same rooms, same angles, same order as move-in. Side-by-side pairs of the same corner, months apart, end almost every argument before it is made.

Also record: meter readings, keys returned and counted, and the forwarding address **in writing** - that last one determines which statutory clock applies to the deposit.

**3. The itemisation.** For each deduction: what it is, the photographic evidence, and an invoice or written quote. Never a round number. "Cleaning - $250" invites a challenge; "Professional clean, invoice attached - $185" does not.

## How does this connect to the deposit deadline?

Directly, and the deadline is unforgiving.

In Connecticut you have **21 days from the end of the tenancy** to either return the deposit with interest, or deliver a written, itemised notice of damages **together with the balance you are not claiming**. If the tenant never gave you a written forwarding address, the clock instead runs **15 days from receiving one**.

Miss it and you may owe **twice the deposit**. The full rules - the two-month cap, escrow at a Connecticut institution, annual interest at 0.49% for 2026 - are in [Connecticut security deposit rules](/blog/connecticut-security-deposit-rules/).

The practical consequence for inspections: **do the move-out inspection the day the tenancy ends, not a week later.** You need time to get quotes, prepare the itemisation and post it inside 21 days. Landlords who lose deposit cases usually lost them by starting the paperwork on day 18.

## What is the equipment I actually need?

Almost none, which is why there is no excuse for skipping it.

- A phone with the date stamp enabled
- A printed condition report, two copies
- A tape measure for anything you may need to quantify
- A torch for under sinks and in cupboards
- A folder, physical or digital, per tenancy

Store everything in one place per tenancy: signed report, move-in photographs, move-out photographs, invoices, correspondence, the forwarding address. If you ever need it, you will need all of it at once.

## What about inspections during the tenancy?

Periodic inspections catch small problems while they are still small - a slow leak under a sink, a failing seal, an unreported issue the tenant has stopped mentioning.

Twice a year is a reasonable rhythm. Give **proper advance notice** in line with your lease and Connecticut's requirement of reasonable notice before entry, keep it brief, and share what you found. A tenant who sees the visit as maintenance rather than surveillance will report problems earlier, which is the entire point.

## The short version

- Photograph everything at move-in, including what looks fine.
- Get a signed condition report, and let the tenant amend it within 72 hours.
- Know the wear-versus-damage line, and account for the age of the item.
- Do a pre-move-out walkthrough two weeks ahead so the tenant can fix things themselves.
- Repeat the same photographs at move-out, same angles.
- Get the forwarding address in writing - it sets the deadline.
- Inspect on the day the tenancy ends, not on day 18.
- Itemise with invoices, never round numbers.
