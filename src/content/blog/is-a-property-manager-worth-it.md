A property manager is worth it when the vacancy they prevent, the rent they recover and the repair pricing they get exceed their fee. On a $2,000/month rental at a 10% fee, that means they need to produce roughly **$2,400 a year in combined value** to break even. In practice the decision usually turns on one number - **vacancy days** - because a single extra month empty costs $2,000, which is ten months of management fee.

This is not a "yes, obviously" or "no, never" question. It is arithmetic, and the arithmetic goes different ways for different owners. Here is how to run it on your own property.

## What are the four numbers that decide it?

Everything else is noise around these four.

**1. Vacancy days.** How long your unit sits empty between tenants, multiplied by daily rent. On a $2,000 rental, every week empty is $460.

**2. Rent positioning.** The gap between what you charge and what comparable units currently let for. Owners who renew at last year's number and never check the market drift below it a little each year.

**3. Repair pricing.** What you pay per job versus what a company with standing trade relationships pays - offset against any markup they add on top.

**4. Your own time.** Hours per month on listings, showings, calls, coordination and paperwork, valued at whatever your time is actually worth.

## How much does vacancy actually cost?

More than the fee, in most cases. That is the whole argument in one line.

| Rent | 1 week empty | 2 weeks | 1 month | 6 weeks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $1,500 | $346 | $692 | $1,500 | $2,077 |
| $2,000 | $462 | $923 | $2,000 | $2,769 |
| $2,500 | $577 | $1,154 | $2,500 | $3,462 |
| $3,000 | $692 | $1,385 | $3,000 | $4,154 |

At $2,000 rent and a 10% fee, the annual management cost is about $2,400. Reading across the table: if a manager fills your unit **five weeks faster** than you would, they have covered their own fee before doing anything else.

Whether they will is a question you can test rather than guess. Ask for their average days-on-market and compare it against how long your last two turnovers actually took. Both numbers exist; neither requires trust.

## What does self-managing really cost?

Not zero. The costs are real, they are just not invoiced.

**Time.** A single tenancy typically runs 4-8 hours a month once you include listing updates, showings, maintenance calls, chasing rent and record-keeping - and much more in a turnover month. At $50/hour of your own time, that is $2,400-$4,800 a year on one unit.

**Repair pricing.** An owner calling a plumber they found online pays list price and waits. A company sending its own technician does not. On $1,800 of annual repairs, the difference is a few hundred dollars either way, depending on how good your own contacts are.

**Rent drift.** If you are $75/month below market and do not know it, that is $900 a year, quietly, forever.

**Legal exposure.** This is the low-probability, high-cost item. Connecticut has specific statutory rules on security deposits - a two-month cap, escrow in a Connecticut financial institution, annual interest, and a 21-day return window - and getting the return wrong can make a landlord liable for twice the deposit. Improper notices and fair housing missteps carry their own exposure. Most owners never trip these. The ones who do rarely do it twice.

## When self-managing is the better call

Honestly, often. A manager is the wrong answer if:

- You live near the property and have flexible daytime hours for showings and contractor access.
- You already have trades you trust and can reach.
- Your tenant is long-term, pays on time, and the unit rarely turns over.
- You enjoy the control, or the rent is low enough that a percentage fee is a large share of thin margins.
- You own one unit and your time genuinely is not the constraint.

Under those conditions the fee buys you very little, because the value a manager adds - speed, pricing power, process - is value you already have.

## When it usually pays for itself

- **You are more than about 30 minutes from the property.** Distance turns every showing and every contractor visit into half a day.
- **Your unit takes more than three weeks to fill.** See the vacancy table.
- **You have three or more doors.** Coordination cost grows faster than door count.
- **You have not benchmarked rent in two years.** There is likely money on the table.
- **Turnover is frequent.** Every turnover is a marketing, screening and legal cycle.
- **You are holding the property as an investment rather than a hobby.** Then your time has an opportunity cost that belongs in the calculation.

## A worked comparison

Same $2,000/month unit, two ways, over one year with one turnover.

| | Self-managed | Managed |
|---|---|---|
| Rent collected | $22,000 (6 weeks vacant) | $23,077 (2 weeks vacant) |
| Rent level | $2,000 | $2,100 after market review |
| Management fee | - | -$2,308 |
| Leasing fee | - | -$1,575 |
| Repairs | -$1,800 | -$1,650 incl. markup |
| Your time (60 hrs @ $50) | -$3,000 | -$400 |
| **Net** | **$17,200** | **$17,144** |

Almost identical - which is the honest answer. Move any single input and it tips: two more weeks of vacancy self-managing and the manager is $900 ahead; value your time at $20/hour instead of $50 and you are $1,800 ahead self-managing.

That is why nobody can answer this question for you from a blog post. **Run the table with your rent, your real vacancy history and your own hourly number.**

## A middle option most owners do not know about

You do not have to choose all or nothing.

- **Tenant placement only.** A manager markets, shows, screens and papers the lease for a flat leasing fee, then hands the tenancy back to you. This buys the part that is hardest to do well alone - screening and speed - without an ongoing percentage.
- **Maintenance coordination only.** You keep the money and the tenant relationship; someone else takes the 11pm call about the boiler.

Both cost a fraction of full management and both are worth asking about, because a lot of companies do not advertise them.

## The one number to check first

Before anything else, work out what your last turnover actually cost you in vacant days. Most owners have never calculated it, and it is usually the largest single number in the whole comparison.
